Appendix

Method, sources and notes

This appendix collects everything about the harmonic analysis of Korngold’s String Quartet No. 2, Op. 26 that is not the analysis itself: how the score was transcribed and analysed, the sources used, the notation conventions and the limits of the readings. Each movement’s page carries only the beat-by-beat readings and the hierarchy of themes, paragraphs and phrases.

What this is

What this analysis is

Each movement’s page records the notes that actually sound in the score, quarter-note beat by quarter-note beat, and reads for every beat a chord, a harmonic function and a tonal context. The beat readings are grouped into three levels: phrases, paragraphs and theme blocks. Chord labels are derived from the sounding notes and the bass; the functional readings and the formal divisions are my analysis. Movements I and IV are complete; movements II and III have not yet been transcribed or analysed.

Sources

Sources

The only primary sources are the scanned score on IMSLP (IMSLP 589869; movement I in two parts, eight pages in all, movement IV thirteen pages) and the MuseScore transcription made from it. No secondary sources, such as recording notes or published analyses, were used.

Transcription

Transcription: method and limits

Movement IV was transcribed by optical recognition of the scan with Audiveris 5.11 and then corrected bar by bar against 400 dpi crops of all 365 bars. No errors are known, but a few decisions were left open. The cello’s slur in mm.186–187 has its arc on one side only and was entered as a tie. The “arco” in Violin II at m.11 and m.129 is an editorial addition for performance and does not affect the harmony. The alla breve sign at m.248 was entered as 2/2.

Movement I originally differed from the printed score in twelve places (the viola clef in m.85; mm.96, 97, 101, 105 and 110–112; the cello accidentals in mm.10–11; the ties in mm.118–119). All were checked against the scan and corrected in the score on 28 September 2026, and the beat table and chord labels were regenerated from the corrected file.

The notes shown in the beat tables are those of the transcription, for both movements. No known differences between the transcription and the print remain.
Method

Method of analysis

The notes of every quarter-note beat were extracted automatically from the transcription’s MusicXML, recording the sounding notes and their lowest note at every change of sonority (489 beats in movement I, 1095 in movement IV). Chord labels were assigned from each beat’s pitch set and bass, and every label was checked mechanically: the notes it names must actually sound and its bass must be the actual lowest note. All beats were then re-examined independently for the grounds of each reading and for alternative readings. Phrase and theme boundaries were set from entries, rests, cadences and thematic returns.

The confidence of each beat (high, medium, low) is kept in the analysis data (JSON and CSV). Where an alternative reading is realistic, the text says “can also be read as”; readings supported by only two voices are marked medium.

Conventions

Notation conventions

  • Beats are counted in quarter notes. The exact half of a beat is written “&” in the prose (beat 3&) and “+1/2” in chord labels; +1/4, +3/4, +1/3 and +2/3 mark other positions inside the beat.
  • Chord labels follow the spelling of the printed score. The note after a slash is the actual lowest note. “no3” and “no5” mean that the third or fifth is not sounding. ø7 is a half-diminished seventh chord, °7 a diminished seventh chord.
  • Roman numerals refer to the tonal context given for the beat. V/x is a secondary dominant and vii°7/x a leading-tone chord. Key names are spelled by function, with the notated spelling in parentheses (for example F♭ major (notated E major)).
  • The number after a pitch name is its octave, with C4 as middle C. Dyads are also written with a slash between the two pitch names; this is not the chord-label slash, and the lower note comes first.
Notes

Notes

The formal divisions (exposition and recapitulation, sonata rondo and so on), the functional reading of each beat and the phrase boundaries are the results of my analysis of the transcription; they do not represent the composer’s intentions or the views of other analysts. Every note a label names is a sounding note, but some functional readings leave room for another reading, and those places are said so in the text.
